DOCUMENT TYPE: Clinic Note  |  DEPARTMENT: Pulmonology

Sarah K. is a 33-year-old female with a longstanding history of moderate persistent asthma and allergic rhinitis who presents today for a follow-up visit. She reports increased frequency of wheezing episodes over the past three weeks, occurring approximately four times per week, along with nocturnal awakenings roughly two to three nights per week. She attributes the worsening to seasonal pollen exposure and a recent upper respiratory tract infection one month prior. She has been using her albuterol rescue inhaler more than daily and notes that it provides only partial relief. Her current maintenance therapy includes fluticasone-salmeterol 250/50 mcg inhaled twice daily, which she states she has been taking consistently. She denies fever, chest pain, hemoptysis, or recent travel. She has a history of allergic rhinitis managed with cetirizine 10 mg daily and fluticasone nasal spray. No prior hospitalizations for asthma. She has a cat at home and reports ongoing exposure to animal dander, which was identified as a trigger on prior allergy testing two years ago. She works as a schoolteacher and notes that classroom dust may also be contributing.

On examination today, her vital signs show a blood pressure of 112/70 mmHg, heart rate of 82 beats per minute, respiratory rate of 16 breaths per minute, temperature 98.2°F, and oxygen saturation of 96% on room air. Auscultation of the lungs reveals mild diffuse expiratory wheeze bilaterally. No accessory muscle use is present. Nasal mucosa is boggy and pale with clear rhinorrhea. Spirometry performed in clinic demonstrates an FEV1 of 68% predicted and an FEV1/FVC ratio of 0.71, with a 14% improvement in FEV1 post-bronchodilator, confirming partially reversible obstruction. Peak flow is 310 L/min against a personal best of 420 L/min. The impression is moderate persistent asthma with suboptimal control, likely worsened by ongoing allergen exposure and the recent viral illness. The plan is to step up therapy by switching to fluticasone-salmeterol 500/50 mcg twice daily, continue albuterol as needed for rescue, and add montelukast 10 mg nightly for additional anti-inflammatory benefit given her concurrent allergic rhinitis. She is counseled extensively on trigger avoidance, particularly regarding the cat and dusty classroom environments. Referral to the allergy and immunology clinic is placed to discuss allergen immunotherapy as a long-term strategy. She is instructed to complete an asthma action plan and return to clinic in six weeks for repeat spirometry. She should go to the emergency department or call 911 if she experiences severe breathlessness, inability to speak in full sentences, or rescue inhaler use every 20 minutes without improvement.
